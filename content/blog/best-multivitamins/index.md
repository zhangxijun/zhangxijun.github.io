Walk into any pharmacy and the multivitamin shelf looks like a wall of identical promises. Most
bottles list the same vitamins, the same percentages and the same words about energy and immune
support. The differences that actually matter are quieter: how many servings you get for the
price, whether the vitamin D is 400 IU or 2,000 IU, which form of folate is listed, and whether
the formula was designed for a 30-year-old man, a 55-year-old woman or a teenager.

We compared ten genuinely popular multivitamins sold on iHerb, read the supplement facts panel on
each one, calculated cost per serving rather than cost per bottle, and collected real buyer
reviews so you can see how each product behaves after a few weeks of use. Every pick answers a
different question, from the cheapest way to cover the basics to the gummy you will actually
finish.

## Why take a multivitamin at all

A multivitamin is not a shortcut for a bad diet, and it will not give you a burst of energy on
day two. What it does is close small, repeated gaps: the vitamin D you do not get from an indoor
job, the B12 that is hard to obtain on a plant-based diet, the iron many women lose every month,
or the calcium and vitamin D that become more important after 50.

That is why the official advice sounds so modest. The goal is to reach the recommended daily
amounts most days, not to take ten times them. A 1,000% dose of vitamin C does not make you
twice as healthy as a 100% dose; it mostly gives your kidneys extra work.

The people who get the clearest benefit are those with a restricted diet, women who are pregnant
or planning to be, adults over 50, vegans and vegetarians, and anyone whose doctor has flagged a
specific shortfall. If you already eat a wide variety of foods and spend time outdoors, a
multivitamin is a small insurance policy rather than a necessity — which is exactly why the price
per serving matters so much. Paying \$1.33 a day for insurance is a different decision from
paying 8 cents.

## What the main vitamins actually do

| Nutrient | What it is for | What to look for on the label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin D | Bone structure, muscle function and normal immune function | 25 mcg (1,000 IU) is a common daily amount; some 50+ formulas use 50 mcg |
| B12 | Red blood cell formation and normal nerve function | 2.4 mcg is the daily value; methylcobalamin is the active form |
| Folate | Normal cell division, especially important before and during pregnancy | 400 mcg DFE; methylfolate is the active form, folic acid the synthetic one |
| Iron | Red blood cell formation | 18 mg covers adult women; most men's formulas leave it out |
| Vitamin C | Antioxidant support and iron absorption from plant foods | 60-90 mg is a normal daily amount, not 1,000 mg |
| Zinc | Normal immune function and wound healing | 8-11 mg is typical; chelated forms are gentler on the stomach |
| Magnesium | Muscle and nerve function | 50-120 mg appears in men's and 50+ formulas |
| Calcium | Bone structure | 200-260 mg is common in multis, far below a dedicated calcium supplement |
| Vitamin K | Normal blood clotting and bone support | 120 mcg (K1) is common; K2 appears in premium formulas |
| Iodine | Normal thyroid function | 150 mcg is the daily value and often missing from cheap formulas |

Two practical notes. First, a multivitamin is not a calcium supplement: 200 mg of calcium covers
only about a fifth of what an adult needs, so it does not replace dairy, fortified foods or a
separate calcium product. Second, percent daily values above 100% are not automatically better.
For vitamin B12 and vitamin D, higher doses exist because absorption is imperfect; for vitamin C
and most B vitamins, a large excess is simply excreted.

## How we picked

- **Popularity you can check** — neither retailer publishes exact unit sales, so we used rating
  counts as a proxy. The picks here range from 606 to 27,000 iHerb ratings.
- **Cost per serving** — price divided by servings, not by tablets in the bottle. A "cheap"
  60-count bottle can cost more per day than a 300-count bottle.
- **Nutrient form and dose** — RDA-level doses for general use, higher vitamin D and B12 for 50+,
  methylated B vitamins where the label states them, and iron only where it belongs.
- **Fit for the buyer** — women, men, 50+, athletes and pill-averse shoppers need different
  formulas, so each pick answers a different question.
- **Real buyer experience** — we read iHerb reviews for each product and included both praise and
  complaints, including the B12 and price objections on the New Chapter bottle and the tablet-size
  comments on Opti-Men.

## FAQ

**Is an expensive multivitamin better than a cheap one?**
Not automatically. A 6-cent RDA tablet and a \$1.33 certified capsule both cover the basics; the
premium price buys cleaner sourcing, active vitamin forms, third-party certification and smaller
batches. If your diet is varied and you just want insurance, the cheap tablet is rational. If you
have absorption issues, restricted eating or you specifically want methylated B vitamins, the
premium bottle can be worth it.

**Should I take a multivitamin with or without food?**
Most formulas are fine either way, but iron, zinc and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are
absorbed better with a meal. If a tablet upsets your stomach, take it with breakfast rather than
on an empty stomach.

**Do I need extra iron?**
Adult women under 50 usually do, and the 18 mg in Centrum or Sentry covers it. Most men and
post-menopausal women do not need a daily iron supplement, which is why One A Day Men's and the
Garden of Life women's formula leave it out. Do not add iron without a blood test that shows low
ferritin.

**Can a multivitamin replace a healthy diet?**
No. It fills gaps; it does not provide fiber, protein, essential fatty acids or the thousands of
compounds in whole foods. Think of it as a small daily top-up, not a replacement.

## A safety note

Multivitamins are generally well tolerated, but more is not better. Vitamin A, iron and zinc can
be harmful in large amounts, and high-dose B6 has been linked to nerve tingling, so compare the B6
figure on the label (50-75 mg in some formulas here) with anything else you take. Check every
supplement label together so you are not doubling up
on the same nutrient, and remember that a multivitamin is not a treatment for fatigue, hair loss
or any medical condition. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking prescription medication or
managing a health condition, talk to your doctor or pharmacist before adding one. This article is
general information, not medical advice.
