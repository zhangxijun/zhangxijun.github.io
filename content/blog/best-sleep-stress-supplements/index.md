Two magnesium bottles, one shelf. Doctor's Best prints 200 mg of elemental magnesium per two tablets and works out to 20 cents a day. Life Extension's L-threonate bottle prints 144 mg and costs 89 cents. The cheaper bottle carries 219,787 iHerb reviews and another 76,803 on Amazon; the expensive one 36,843 and 24,901.

The same size type sits on both fronts. The difference lives on the back, in a number most shoppers never check: elemental magnesium, not the weight of the compound. Two thousand milligrams of magnesium glycinate chelate yields 200 mg of magnesium, a 10:1 ratio. A bottle that says "500 mg magnesium glycinate" is not selling you 500 mg of magnesium.

Melatonin has the opposite problem. A 300 mcg capsule and a 5 mg tablet sit on the same shelf and differ by a factor of sixteen, and the smaller dose is the one the circadian studies used. Three of the ten picks below are magnesium and two are melatonin because that is what the shelf actually contains. The rest answer different questions: how to take the edge off a stressful afternoon without sedation, how to bring cortisol down over weeks, what to take when melatonin leaves you foggy at 7 a.m.

## Why take a sleep supplement at all

The first-line treatment for insomnia is not a bottle. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia outperforms hypnotics over the long run, and about a third of US adults report sleeping less than seven hours against a recommendation of seven to nine. A capsule cannot fix sleep apnea, a 2 a.m. phone habit, or a bedroom at 26°C.

What a few of them can do is narrower and more useful. Magnesium does almost nothing if your intake is already adequate; national diet surveys put a large share of adults below the estimated average requirement of roughly 265 to 350 mg a day, and the sleep benefit shows up mostly in that group. Melatonin does not sedate you — it shifts the timing of a body clock that is running late, which is why it works for jet lag and shift work and disappoints people who expect a sleeping pill. Theanine blunts the arousal side of caffeine and stress without making you drowsy. Ashwagandha has small trials behind cortisol and sleep over six to eight weeks, not one night.

That difference in mechanism is why cost per serving decides whether a trial is worth running. Melatonin at 300 mcg costs 8 cents a night, or \$7.50 for three months. Magnesium L-threonate costs 89 cents, or about \$80 for the same three months, for a third of the elemental dose. If you are testing a hypothesis — am I short on magnesium? — the cheap form answers it faster.

## What the main sleep ingredients actually do

| Ingredient | What it is for | What to check on the label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elemental magnesium | Muscle relaxation and sleep quality when intake is low | mg of elemental magnesium per serving, not mg of compound |
| Magnesium glycinate | The gentle form, least likely to loosen stool | 200 mg elemental per 2 capsules or tablets |
| Magnesium citrate | Higher dose per tablet, predictable laxative effect | 400 mg per 2 tablets on the NOW bottle |
| Magnesium L-threonate | Marketed for magnesium in the brain | 144 mg elemental per 3 capsules, the priciest form |
| Melatonin | Shifts the body clock; it is not a sedative | mcg on the label, not mg — 300 mcg is a physiological dose |
| L-theanine | Calm alertness, blunts caffeine jitters | 100-200 mg, and whether inositol is added |
| Ashwagandha | Stress and cortisol over weeks | 300-600 mg KSM-66, standardized to 5% withanolides |
| Glycine | Lowers core temperature before sleep | 3 g per serving, almost always a powder |
| Valerian | Sedating herb with mixed trial results | 0.8% valerenic acids, 400-900 mg per serving |

The compound-versus-elemental trap is the most expensive mistake in the category. Two tablets of NOW's magnesium citrate carry 400 mg of magnesium because the label counts the element. Two tablets of a 1,000 mg "magnesium glycinate" that does not print elemental magnesium might carry 100 mg. Read the Supplement Facts panel, not the front of the bottle, and compare elemental milligrams.

Melatonin has the reverse problem: bigger numbers sell better and do less. A 0.3 mg dose brings blood melatonin up to the range your body makes on its own at night. Five milligrams pushes it ten to twenty times higher, and the trials do not show better sleep onset at that level — they show more next-day fuzziness and more vivid dreams. If you wake at 3 a.m. rather than failing to fall asleep, dose is the wrong lever anyway; timing and a time-release format matter more.

**If you only buy one:** Doctor's Best at 20 cents a day is the safest first bottle - 200 mg of chelated magnesium, 219,787 iHerb reviews, and no sedative effect to test-drive. If your problem is falling asleep at the wrong hour rather than low magnesium, the \$7.50 melatonin is the cheaper experiment.

## How we picked

- **Elemental dose, then the form** — every magnesium pick was compared on elemental milligrams per serving, because the compound weight on the front of the bottle says nothing about the dose. The form came second: glycinate for a calm stomach, citrate for a bigger dose, threonate for the brain claim.
- **Cost per serving, not per bottle** — the \$32.39 bottle of 30 servings and the \$7.50 bottle of 100 are not in the same price class once you divide. The cheapest pick here is 8 cents a night; the most expensive is \$1.08, a thirteenfold gap for products sold on the same shelf.
- **Amazon category ranks, then the label** — sleep supplements, magnesium and stress formulas were checked against Amazon's best-seller lists to catch mainstream sellers that an iHerb-only search would miss. Rank tells you what is selling; the Supplement Facts panel tells you what you are swallowing.
- **A review base big enough to show a pattern** — 300 ratings can be noise; 19,000 cannot. Quotes were copied from iHerb verified purchases and Amazon listings, complaints included, and the critical ones stayed in.
- **One working retailer per button** — no search links and no listings without a live offer. Where the Amazon buy box belonged to an unrelated third-party seller, the card carries one iHerb button instead.

## FAQ

**How long before any of this works?** Melatonin works the first night on sleep timing, not on sleep depth. Theanine takes about an hour. Glycine and magnesium, if you are short, show up within a week. Ashwagandha is the slow one: four to six weeks at 600 mg is the timeline the trials used, and the 300 mg capsule means two a night.

**Can I take magnesium and melatonin together?** Yes, they do different jobs — one is a mineral your body uses, the other is a timing signal. Take the melatonin four to five hours before target bedtime for jet lag, or 30 minutes before for shift work, and the magnesium with dinner. Start magnesium at one serving rather than the full 400 mg if you have never taken it.

**Is 5 mg melatonin too much?** For most adults it is more than the research supports, not dangerous. The physiological dose is 0.3 mg, and 5 mg raises blood levels well above what your pineal gland produces. It is a reasonable dose for a shift worker who must sleep against the clock; for ordinary difficulty falling asleep, start at 300 mcg.

**Do I need the expensive magnesium forms?** Only if you have a specific reason. L-threonate costs 89 cents for 144 mg; the glycinate picks cost 17 to 20 cents for 200 mg. If the goal is correcting a low intake, the cheap chelate does that. If the goal is the brain-magnesium claim, you are paying for a hypothesis, and should know that going in.

## A safety note

Melatonin is not for everyone. It can interact with blood thinners, immunosuppressants, diabetes medication and birth control, and it is generally avoided in pregnancy and in children without a clinician's advice. Stop if you get morning grogginess that lasts past breakfast or vivid dreams that wake you.

Magnesium is excreted by the kidneys, so anyone with reduced kidney function should not take a supplement without asking a doctor. High doses cause loose stools; that is the main reason to start low and build. Ashwagandha has been linked to rare liver injury and can affect thyroid hormone levels, so it is off the list during pregnancy and for anyone with thyroid disease or a liver condition. Valerian adds to the effect of alcohol, benzodiazepines and other sedatives, and it makes driving unsafe.

None of these products treat insomnia, sleep apnea or an anxiety disorder. If you have been sleeping badly for more than three months, or you snore heavily and wake unrefreshed, the useful next step is a clinician, not another bottle. This article is general information, not medical advice.
